# Somerton Man — Verified Fact Sheet

Every fact below is checked against the Wikipedia article "Somerton Man" (retrieved 11 Oct 2026),
cross-checked with news coverage (The Independent, Smithsonian Magazine, ABC, Herald Sun) and the
Cipher Foundation. Anything not verified is marked UNSPECIFIED and is NOT used in the film.

## Discovery
- 1 December 1948, ~6:30 am: body found on Somerton Park beach near Glenelg, ~11 km southwest of Adelaide,
  opposite the Crippled Children's Home (corner of The Esplanade and Bickford Terrace).
- Found by John Lyons and two men (The Advertiser, 2 Dec 1948: discovery by "Mr J. Lyons, of Whyte Rd, Somerton").
- Lying back, head resting against the seawall, legs extended, feet crossed. Believed at first he had died sleeping.
- A half-smoked cigarette rested on the right collar of his coat.
- Pockets contained: an unused second-class rail ticket from Adelaide to Henley Beach; a bus ticket from the
  city that may not have been used; a narrow aluminium comb manufactured in the US; a half-empty packet of
  Juicy Fruit chewing gum; an Army Club cigarette packet containing seven cigarettes of a different brand,
  Kensitas; a quarter-full box of Bryant & May matches. No wallet, no identification, no hat (unusual in 1948).

## The evening before
- Witnesses saw an individual resembling the dead man lying on his back in the same spot on 30 November.
- A couple who saw him ~7 pm saw him extend his right arm to its fullest extent and then drop it limply.
- Another couple, 7:30–8 pm (street lights on), did not see him move for half an hour; found it odd he was
  not reacting to mosquitoes; assumed drunk or asleep.
- One witness told police she observed a man looking down at the sleeping man from the top of the steps to the beach.
- 1959: a witness reported he and three others had seen a well-dressed man carrying another man on his
  shoulders along Somerton Park beach the night before the body was found (police report by Detective Don O'Doherty).

## The body
- Pathologist John Burton Cleland: "Britisher" appearance, aged about 40–45, "top physical condition".
- 180 cm tall; grey eyes; fair to ginger-coloured hair, slightly grey at the temples; broad shoulders, narrow waist;
  hands and nails showed no signs of manual labour; big and little toes met in a wedge shape (like a dancer or a
  wearer of pointed-toe boots); pronounced high calf muscles (high-heeled boots or ballet).
- Clothes: white shirt; red, white and blue tie; brown trousers; socks and shoes; brown knitted pullover; a
  fashionable grey and brown double-breasted jacket of reportedly "American" tailoring.
- All labels on his clothes had been removed. Clean-shaven. Dental records could not be matched to any known person.
- Body embalmed 10 December 1948 after police failed to identify him.

## Autopsy and inquest
- Estimated time of death ~2 am, 1 December (a "quick opinion" on rigor mortis; poisons affect rigor, so it is
  probably inaccurate). Last meal: a pasty eaten about 3–4 hours before death (~10–11 pm).
- Findings: congestion of the brain, pharynx; whitening/ulceration of the gullet mucosa; deeply congested stomach
  with blood mixed with food; congestion in duodenum, both kidneys, liver (great excess of blood); spleen
  strikingly large, about 3 times normal size; destruction of the centre of the liver lobules under the microscope;
  acute gastritis haemorrhage.
- Tests failed to reveal any foreign substance in the body.
- Pathologist John Dwyer: "I am quite convinced the death could not have been natural ... the poison I suggested
  was a barbiturate or a soluble hypnotic."
- Inquest by coroner Thomas Erskine Cleland, opened December 1948, adjourned to 17 June 1949.
- Cleland: shoes "remarkably clean" and recently polished — inconsistent with a man wandering Glenelg all day;
  this fitted the theory the body may have been taken to the beach after death. He stressed this was speculation;
  witnesses believed it was "definitely the same person" (same place, same distinctive position).
- Cedric Stanton Hicks, professor of physiology and pharmacology: two drugs ("number 1", "number 2") extremely
  toxic in small oral doses, extremely difficult to detect. Names sealed as Exhibit C.18; not released until the
  1980s; later identified as digitalis and ouabain (cardiac glycosides). Only "fact" not found: vomiting.
- Cleland: "I would be prepared to find that he died from poison, that the poison was probably a glucoside and
  that it was not accidentally administered; but I cannot say whether it was administered by the deceased himself
  or by some other person." The coroner could not determine identity or cause of death.
- After the inquest a plaster cast of the man's head and shoulders was made. Media called it "one of Australia's
  most profound mysteries".

## The suitcase
- 14 January 1949: Adelaide railway station staff found a brown suitcase, label removed, checked into the
  cloakroom after 11:00 am on 30 November 1948. Believed to be his.
- Contents: red checked dressing gown; size-seven red felt slippers; four pairs of underpants; pyjamas; shaving
  items; light brown trousers with sand in the cuffs; an electrician's screwdriver; a table knife cut down into a
  short sharp instrument; scissors with sharpened points; a small square of zinc (likely a sheath for knife and
  scissors); a stencilling brush of the kind used by third officers on merchant ships.
- A thread card of Barbour orange waxed thread, "an unusual type" not available in Australia — the same thread
  used to repair the lining of a pocket of the trousers the dead man was wearing.
- All identification marks removed EXCEPT: "T. Keane" on a tie, "Keane" on a laundry bag, "Kean" on a singlet;
  three dry-cleaning marks: 1171/7, 4393/7, 3053/7. No "T. Keane" was missing in any English-speaking country;
  circulation of the dry-cleaning marks proved fruitless.
- The coat's front gusset and featherstitching indicated US manufacture; it had not been imported — so he had
  been to America, or acquired the coat from someone of similar size who had.
- Police believed he arrived by overnight train from Melbourne, Sydney or Port Augusta; possibly showered and
  shaved at the City Baths (no Baths ticket on the body); bought a ticket for the 10:50 am Henley Beach train but
  did not board; checked the suitcase; caught a city bus to Glenelg.
- No spare socks and no correspondence in the case; pencils and unused letter stationery were found.
- The suitcase and contents were destroyed in 1986 as "no longer required".

## Tamám Shud
- Around the time of the inquest (police timeline: 6–14 June 1949), a tiny rolled-up paper printed "Tamám Shud"
  was found in a concealed fob pocket sewn inside the trousers' pocket.
- Persian tamám shud (تمام شد): "ended" / "finished" / "it is over" — the last words of the Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam
  (Edward FitzGerald's translation). The paper's verso was blank; police searched Australia-wide for a copy with a
  blank verso; a photograph of the scrap was released to the press.
- 22 July 1949: a man handed police a copy — a 1941 edition of FitzGerald's translation, published by Whitcombe
  and Tombs, Christchurch, New Zealand. Detective Sergeant Lionel Leane protected witnesses with pseudonyms; the
  finder was called "Ronald Francis" and has never been officially identified.
- The book was found in an unlocked car parked on Jetty Road, Glenelg (rear floor well or back seat — accounts
  differ). Timing is uncertain: one newspaper says a week or two before the body was found; cold-case detective
  Gerry Feltus says "just after" the body was found. "Francis" only connected it to the case after a newspaper
  article the previous day.
- Microscopic tests matched the scrap to the torn page. The Rubaiyat's theme (live fully, no regrets at the end)
  led some police to theorise suicide; at the inquest police gave the paper no significance; nothing else
  corroborated the theory.

## The code
- Faint pencil indentations in the back of the book: five lines of capital letters, plus a local telephone number
  and another unidentified number.
- Text (widely rendered):
  WRGOABABD / MLIAOI (struck out) / WTBIMPANETP / MLIABOAIAQC / ITTMTSAMSTGAB
  The second line is struck out — considered significant (similar to the fourth line; possibly an encryption
  error). An "X" appears above the last "O" of the fourth line. The first letter is M or W (widely believed W).
- 1978: Department of Defence cryptographers (for ABC TV): impossible to give "a satisfactory answer"; too few
  symbols; could be the "meaningless" product of a "disturbed mind".
- 2004: Gerry Feltus suggested the last line could be initials of "It's Time To Move To South Australia Moseley
  Street..." (Jessica Thomson lived on Moseley Street).
- 2009–2011: Derek Abbott's team: most likely each letter is the first letter of a word.
- 2014: computational linguist John Rehling: supports "initials of English text", but finds no match in a large
  survey of literature; concludes likely shorthand, not a code; the original can likely never be determined.
- The original book was lost in the 1950s. The code has never been deciphered to the satisfaction of authorities.

## Jessica Thomson and Alf Boxall
- The phone number belonged to nurse Jessica Ellen "Jo" Thomson (1921–2007), born Jessie Harkness (Marrickville,
  Sydney), living in Moseley Street, Glenelg — about 400 m from where the body was found.
- She said she did not know the dead man or why he would have her number; she also reported that in late 1948 an
  unidentified man had attempted to visit her and asked a neighbour about her.
- 1949: she asked police not to keep a permanent record of her name; they agreed — a decision that hampered later
  investigations. She was referred to by pseudonyms, including "Jestyn". Her real name was not established until 2002.
- Shown the plaster cast by DS Leane, she could not identify the man. Leane: she was "completely taken aback, to
  the point of giving the appearance that she was about to faint". Paul Lawson (the technician who made the cast,
  present at the viewing): she immediately looked away and would not look at it again.
- Cold-case detective Gerry Feltus (interviewed her 2002) found her "evasive" or unwilling to talk; Feltus believed
  she knew the Somerton man's identity. Her daughter Kate (2014, 60 Minutes) said she believed her mother knew him.
- In WWII she worked at Royal North Shore Hospital, Sydney, and owned a copy of the Rubaiyat; in August 1945 she
  gave it to Army lieutenant Alf Boxall (Water Transport Section, Royal Australian Engineers) at the Clifton
  Gardens Hotel, Sydney, inscribed "JEstyn" with verse 70 written out.
- Police suspected Boxall was the dead man; in July 1949 he was found alive in Sydney, his copy (a 1924 Sydney
  edition) intact, "Tamam Shud" still in place. No evidence of any contact between them after 1945.

## Early identifications (context)
- The Advertiser, 2 Dec 1948, p. 3: "Body found on Beach" — believed to be E.C. Johnson of Payneham. Johnson
  walked into a police station alive on 3 December.
- 4 Dec: fingerprints not on SA police records. 5 Dec: a claim of a military pension card in the name
  "Solomonson" (unverified). Jan 1949: body misidentified as woodcutter Robert Walsh; identification retracted.
- A June 1949 journalist: the Somerton Man "seems to have made certain that the glass would be empty, save for
  speculation."

## The Abbott investigation and DNA
- March 2009: University of Adelaide team led by Derek Abbott begins studying the case. Code letter frequency
  differs from random letters; code's format resembles the Rubaiyat's quatrain structure → one-time-pad theory
  (would require the exact edition, which is lost).
- The 1948/49 autopsy reports are now missing.
- Anatomist Maciej Henneberg: the man's cymba (upper ear hollow) larger than his cavum — a feature of 1–2% of the
  Caucasian population. May 2009: dental experts conclude hypodontia (missing lateral incisors) — about 2% of the
  population. June 2010: Jessica Thomson's eldest son Robin had both features; coincidence odds estimated one in
  10–20 million; media speculated about parentage. DNA analysis later linked Robin's daughter to Prosper Thomson's
  grandparents, eliminating the speculation.
- December 2017: three hairs "at the right development stage" found on the plaster death cast, submitted to the
  Australian Centre for Ancient DNA. February 2018: mitochondrial DNA analysed — haplogroup H4a1a1a (about 1% of
  Europeans). mtDNA is maternal only.
- October 2019: Attorney-General Vickie Chapman grants exhumation approval. 19 May 2021: body exhumed (part of
  Operation Persevere / Operation Persist); remains in "reasonable" condition.
- 26 July 2022: Abbott and forensic genealogist Colleen M. Fitzpatrick announce the man was Carl "Charles" Webb,
  electrical engineer and instrument maker born 16 November 1905 in Footscray, Melbourne — based on DNA from hair
  strands preserved in the plaster death mask, and investigative genetic genealogy (matches to descendants of two
  distant cousins, paternal and maternal).
- South Australia Police and Forensic Science South Australia have NOT verified the identification; police said
  they were "cautiously optimistic that this may provide a breakthrough". FSSA declined to comment.
- No pre-death photograph of Webb was known at first; a 1921 Swinburne Technical College football-team photo
  includes a "C. Webb" (not a direct identification); November 2022: ABC's Australian Story published 1920s
  photographs from a Webb family album.

## Carl Webb (as claimed by Abbott/Fitzpatrick)
- Born 16 November 1905, Footscray (Melbourne), youngest of six children. Father Richard August Webb (1866–1939)
  emigrated from Hamburg, Germany; mother Eliza Amelia Morris Grace (1871–1946). Family ran a bakery in Springvale;
  after it closed, Webb retrained as an electrical instrument maker.
- 4 October 1941: married Dorothy "Doff" Robertson, a pharmacist and chiropodist; flat in Bromby Street, South Yarra.
- Dorothy later said the marriage was not harmonious; he was solitary, quiet, in bed by 7 pm, but moody, violent
  and threatening. He was fond of poetry and wrote poems "mostly on the subject of death, which he claims to be
  his greatest desire" (consistent with the Rubaiyat's themes). Abbott: he enjoyed betting on horses — one theory
  is the code could be horse names.
- March 1946: attempted suicide with an overdose of ether, per Dorothy; September 1946: she fled, alleging years
  of abuse. He moved out in 1947; no official records of his whereabouts after that. Family mentioned Cottesloe
  (Perth). By 1951 Dorothy lived in Bute, South Australia (144 km from Adelaide); Abbott speculates he may have
  come to Adelaide to find her. Divorce: filed 5 June 1951, granted April 1952.
- Deaths in seven years: father (1939), brother Roy (1943, died in Burma as a POW of the Thai–Burma railway),
  nephew John Keane (1943, RAAF, UK), mother Eliza (1946). Abbott/Fitzpatrick believe he "spiralled down" after
  these losses and suggest suicide by self-poisoning.
- Brother-in-law John Keane had US-linked possessions (coins, a map of Chicago — possibly from aircrew training in
  Canada en route to the UK); Keane and Webb lived a 20-minute drive apart. This may explain the American-tailored
  clothes and the "Keane" name tags.

## Burial
- Buried in 1949 at West Terrace Cemetery, Adelaide, under a marker reading "Here lies the unknown man who was
  found at Somerton Beach." Body exhumed 19 May 2021 for DNA testing.

## Status of the case
- The code has never been deciphered to the satisfaction of authorities. The cause of death was never determined.
- The Webb identification has not been officially verified by SA Police or Forensic Science South Australia
  (as of the latest available reporting).
